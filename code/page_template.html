The conquest in castles

After Hastings the Normans held England with earth and timber, then stone. I mapped every castle site Wikidata knows in Britain, dated the ones I could, and asked how fast the castle frontier moved, where it bunched, and what Domesday Book says it cost.

In October 1066 William of Normandy won a battle in Sussex. Over the next generations his followers built castles from Cornwall to the Clyde: mounds of earth with timber towers, ringed banks, and later great keeps of stone. Many still stand, as grassy humps in fields or as ruins on hills.

I wanted to see the conquest as a map that grows. Wikidata, the open knowledge base behind Wikipedia, records {{n_items|int}} castle sites of the Norman kinds in England, Wales and Scotland: {{n_by_type.motte|int}} mottes and motte-and-bailey castles, {{n_by_type.ringwork|int}} ringworks, {{n_by_type.keep|int}} keeps and {{n_by_type.castle|int}} sites classed simply as castles. The catch is the dates. Only {{n_dated_any|int}} of them ({{share_dated_any|pct}}) have any date of foundation at all, and only {{n_window|int}} fall between 1066 and 1199.

So this is a story told twice: once with the few castles that carry a date, and once with the many that do not. And it is checked against the one firm witness from the time, Domesday Book, which in 1086 names {{n_domesday_castles|int}} castles in its text.

02 · The paceA frontier that leapt, not crept

If the Normans had conquered like a slow tide, castle dates would grow later the further you go from the battlefield. They do not. The dated castles of the 1060s were already spread across the kingdom: {{decades.0.n|int}} of them, with a median distance of {{decades.0.median_km|int}} km from Battle. By {{furthest_by_1069.year|raw}} the furthest, {{furthest_by_1069.label|raw}}, was {{furthest_by_1069.km|int}} km away. By {{furthest_by_1072_ew.year|raw}} {{furthest_by_1072_ew.label|raw}} stood {{furthest_by_1072_ew.km|int}} km north.

Of the {{first_years_n|int}} castles dated 1066 to 1072, {{first_years_over_300|int}} were already more than 300 km from the battlefield.

The castles did not follow the conquest outwards. They were the conquest, planted at once in the county towns and river crossings of the whole kingdom.

03 · Who gets a dateThe dated castles are the famous ones

Before trusting any of this, I tested whether the dated castles are a fair sample of the rest. They are not. In England and Wales, {{bias_n_dated|int}} castle sites have a date (of any period) and {{bias_n_undated|int}} do not.

Dated against undated castle sites, England and Wales

{{bias.grade1.dated|pct}} of the dated sites are Grade I listed buildings, against {{bias.grade1.undated|pct}} of the undated: about {{bias_grade1_ratio|x0}} times as likely. Only {{bias.earth.dated|pct}} of dated sites are earthworks (mottes or ringworks), against {{bias.earth.undated|pct}} of the undated ones. Dated sites are also far more often among the castles Domesday names ({{bias.dom.dated|pct1}} against {{bias.dom.undated|pct1}}). In short, Wikidata dates the big stone and royal castles, and leaves {{bias.earth.undated_n|int}} grassy mounds and ringworks undated.

The good news for the frontier question is that the bias is about kind, not place. The median dated site is {{bias_dist_median_dated|int}} km from Battle and the median undated site {{bias_dist_median_undated|int}} km. So the dated sample says something fair about how far and how fast, and little about how many.

Even Domesday's witnesses go undated. Of the {{dc_matched|int}} Domesday castles I could match by name to a Wikidata item, {{dc_matched_undated|int}} have no date in Wikidata at all, among them Hastings Castle itself, Cambridge, Gloucester, Richard's Castle and Penwortham. Of the matched items, {{dc_matched_before1066_n|int}} carry a date before 1066 ({{dc_matched_before1066.0.castle|raw}} {{dc_matched_before1066.0.wd_year|raw}} and {{dc_matched_before1066.1.castle|raw}} {{dc_matched_before1066.1.wd_year|raw}}), which I keep as recorded but leave out of the time series.

04 · DomesdayWhat the castles cost, in 1086

Domesday Book was compiled twenty years after Hastings, and it mentions castles as a matter of fact: who holds one, what land it sits on, which houses it replaced. Reading the entries the Hull Domesday Project flags as mentioning a castle, and keeping only those where the 1086 text speaks of a castle at that place (not just a modern name such as Castle Hedingham), gives {{n_domesday_castles|int}} castles. Add the dated Wikidata castles of 1066 to 1086 and I count {{known_by_1086_total|int}} castles known to be standing by 1086.

Did the countryside around castles suffer too? I took every located Domesday place ({{dp_n|int}}) and compared those within {{near_km|int}} km of a castle known by 1086 ({{near_n|int}} places) with the rest. Near castles, {{near_waste|pct1}} of places have an entry calling land waste, against {{far_waste|pct1}} elsewhere. But much of that gap is geography: castles and waste both crowd into the north and the Welsh border. Comparing places within the same county, the gap shrinks to {{near_waste_diff_pts|f1}} percentage points. The median place near a castle was worth {{near_value_median_ratio|f2}} times its 1066 value in 1086, and the median place elsewhere {{far_value_median_ratio|f2}} times. I read this as no clear local footprint: the damage Domesday records is regional, not a ring around each castle.

05 · The Harrying of the NorthWaste where the castles went north

The regional pattern is stark. In 1069 and 1070 William crushed a northern rising by laying the land waste. Domesday, sixteen years later, still shows it. Of {{yorks_n|int}} located Yorkshire places, {{yorks_waste|pct}} have an entry calling land waste, against {{rest_waste|pct1}} in the rest of the surveyed counties. Yorkshire's recorded value in 1086 was {{yorks_value_ratio|pct}} of its 1066 value, where the rest of England, taken together, stood at {{rest_value_ratio|pct}}.

Yorkshire is not top of the list. {{dom_county_waste.0.countyName|raw}} is, with {{dom_county_waste.0.waste|pct}} of places touched by waste, followed by {{dom_county_waste.1.countyName|raw}} ({{dom_county_waste.1.waste|pct}}) and {{dom_county_waste.2.countyName|raw}} ({{dom_county_waste.2.waste|pct}}). Cheshire was harried in the same campaign, and the other two are the Welsh border counties. Wigmore and Clifford castles were, in Domesday's words, built "on waste land". The four most wasted counties of Domesday are the two great castle frontiers: the north and the March of Wales.

06 · The Welsh MarchThe densest castle country in Britain

Count every castle site, dated or not, and divide by land area. A band {{march_band_km|int}} km either side of the England and Wales border covers {{march_band_area_share|pct}} of the land of the two countries but holds {{march_band_share|pct}} of their castle sites, and {{march_band_earth_share|pct}} of their mottes and ringworks. 07 · The north-westLancashire: late, but not that late

The land between the Ribble and the Mersey appears in Domesday as an appendix to Cheshire, and most of the far north-west not at all. 08 · ScotlandA different kind of arrival

Scotland was never conquered by the Normans, but Anglo-Norman lords settled there in the twelfth century and built mottes. Wikidata holds {{scot_n_items|int}} Scottish castle sites of these types, only {{scot_earth|int}} of them mottes or ringworks. Just {{window_by_nation.Scotland|int}} have a date before 1200, from {{scot_window.1.label|raw}} ({{scot_window.1.year|raw}}, a round year) to {{scot_window.6.label|raw}} ({{scot_window.6.year|raw}}). The earliest, {{scot_window.0.label|raw}} at {{scot_window.0.year|raw}}, may not be a Norman castle at all. Most of Scotland's "castles" in Wikidata are later tower castles, so I treat Scotland as context, not evidence.

09 · A checkDoes OpenStreetMap see the same castles?

As a coverage check I took OpenStreetMap's castle features in Britain ({{osm_n|int}}). {{osm_within_1km_share|pct}} of them lie within 1 km of a Wikidata castle site in my set. The other way round, only {{wd_with_osm_1km_share|pct}} of Wikidata sites have an OpenStreetMap castle within 1 km, and only {{wd_with_osm_1km_share_earth|pct}} of the mottes and ringworks. OpenStreetMap maps the standing castles; the earthworks live mostly in heritage lists, which is what Wikidata draws on. Neither is complete, and the gaps differ by country.

How I did this

Castles. From a Wikidata extract (retrieved 27 October 2026) of UK items with coordinates, I kept items classed as castle, motte-and-bailey castle, motte, ringwork castle, shell keep, keep, castle ruin, enclosure castle or siege castle, deduplicated by item, and placed each in a county with ONS boundaries. Items classed only as tower houses, fortified manor houses, quadrangular castles, Welsh princes' castles or other later forms were left out; {{also_later_class_n|int}} items carry one of those classes as well as a castle class and stay in. Northern Ireland was left out. That gives {{n_items|int}} sites. Many "castle" items are later than the Normans; the type filter on the map lets you look at the earthworks alone.

Dates. A castle's date is its earliest Wikidata inception (P571). I never add dates of my own. Wikidata stores a year for dates of all precisions, so a castle "of the 12th century" can appear as 1100 or 1101; I flag years ending in 00 or 01 and test the results without them. In all, {{n_pre1066|int}} items carry dates before 1066 and {{n_after1199|int}} after 1199; both are kept in the data but left out of the time series.

Distance. Straight-line distance in British National Grid from Battle, East Sussex (OS Open Names), the site of the battle.

Domesday. Entries come from the Hull Domesday Project's coded Domesday via the realm-domesday repository. I kept {{n_domesday_castles|int}} castles where the translated 1086 text speaks of a castle at or of a place, placed at the Domesday place's grid reference (Penwortham, which has none in the source, at its Wikidata coordinates). I matched them to Wikidata only where the item's name is the same castle and it lies within 8 km ({{dc_matched|int}} matched, {{dc_unmatched|int}} left unmatched as ambiguous). Waste is the source's flag for entries that call land waste; values are the 1066 and 1086 values of entries that give both, shared equally among the places an entry describes.

Density. Sites per 1,000 km² of land, by county (ceremonial counties in England, preserved counties in Wales, council areas in Scotland) and by hexagon. The March band is all land within {{march_band_km|int}} km of the England and Wales border.

Known problems. Wikidata coverage is uneven: the Welsh and English heritage lists feed it differently, and Scotland's castle items are mostly later. The dated sample favours big, listed castles. Domesday is silent on most castles, says nothing of the far north, and its Yorkshire entries in the source have no English text. Distances from Battle are a rough measure of a conquest that moved by road and river.
